# Core and Accessory Antimicrobial Resistance

## Definition

**Core AMR** comprises resistance gene clusters present broadly across a species’ genomes, whereas **accessory AMR** comprises clusters present in only a subset of genomes. The environmental-resistome analysis operationalizes this distinction with a 95% prevalence threshold, so labels depend on genome sampling and the number of genomes available for each species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The strain-variation analysis uses the same logic within species: genes present in at least 95% of strains are fixed, those present in 5–95% are variable, and those present in at most 5% are rare. [src: amr_strain_variation] This connects [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] with [[concepts/environmental-resistome]]: AMR burden is not only the number of resistance genes, but also their distribution across genomes, strains, environments, and lineages. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_strain_variation]

The pan-bacterial AMR atlas extends this framework to 14,723 species and 82,908 AMR clusters, while the metal-fitness atlas, its metal-specificity follow-up, and the SNIPE survey provide important comparators. Unlike the AMR catalog, which is generally depleted in core genes, broad metal-fitness-important genes are strongly core-enriched. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, metal_fitness_atlas] The SNIPE defense system provides a further example of a predominantly accessory defense function: 86.7% of DUF4041-containing SNIPE clusters were accessory or singleton. [src: snipe_defense_system] These differences indicate that annotated resistance genes, mobile defense systems, and genes required for stress survival are overlapping but non-equivalent biological sets. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, snipe_defense_system]

The metal-specificity analysis makes this distinction more explicit. Among 7,609 metal-important gene records with usable fitness matrices across 24 organisms, 4,177 (54.9%) were metal-specific, 2,888 (38.0%) were generally sick across conditions, and 544 (7.2%) were both metal-associated and broadly stress-sensitive. [src: metal_specificity] Thus, the high core fraction of the broad metal-fitness set cannot be assumed to describe dedicated metal-resistance determinants alone. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, metal_specificity]

A DvH field-versus-laboratory fitness analysis adds a related but distinct perspective: pangenome conservation of genes with strong fitness effects does not cleanly distinguish ecological from laboratory conditions. Field-relevant and laboratory nutrient functions can both be highly conserved, whereas antibiotic- and heavy-metal-associated fitness-important genes can be relatively accessory. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

SNIPE also illustrates why accessory status should not be equated automatically with a particular mechanism. DUF4041/PF13250 homologues were detected in 1,696 species across 33 phyla, but domain detection alone may include non-defense proteins and may miss divergent homologues. [src: snipe_defense_system] The distribution is consistent with [[concepts/horizontal-gene-transfer]] and [[concepts/mobile-genetic-elements]], but the survey did not directly establish the physical mobility of individual SNIPE loci. [src: snipe_defense_system]

## Main evidence

Across 14,723 species and 82,908 AMR gene clusters, clinical species had a substantially more accessory resistome than soil and aquatic species. Clinical species averaged **32.4% core AMR and 67.6% accessory AMR**, compared with **57.1% core and 42.9% accessory AMR** in soil species and **54.4% core and 45.6% accessory AMR** in aquatic species. Human-gut species had the highest accessory fraction, at **80.3%**. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The environment-associated difference in core AMR percentage was significant (Kruskal–Wallis H = 506.0, p = 4×10⁻¹⁰⁷, η² = 0.036). The evidence supports an ecological gradient in which clinical and gut-associated species carry more variably distributed AMR, while soil and aquatic species retain a larger broadly distributed component. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The pan-bacterial atlas independently finds the same overall direction: only **30.3%** of AMR genes are core, compared with **46.8%** for the pangenome baseline (OR=0.49, chi-squared=23,117, p≈0). The auxiliary genome is **2.2x enriched** for AMR (33.6% versus 15.3%), and in a paired analysis of 4,252 species, 63.7% had a lower AMR core fraction than their species baseline (Wilcoxon p=1.1e-130; mean difference -0.102). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

This depletion varies by resistance mechanism. Beta-lactamases were **54.9% core**, regulatory genes were only **6.5% core**, and named mobile examples including blaTEM, tet(C), and ant(2'')-Ia were **0% core**. Intrinsic efflux systems such as emhABC were reported as greater than 95% core, whereas acquired efflux genes were accessory. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The strain-level survey quantifies the same broad pattern within species. Across 1,305 species and 180,025 genomes, 51.3% of AMR gene-species occurrences were rare, 41.3% were variable, and only 7.5% were fixed. The median variability index was 0.526 and the median pairwise Jaccard distance between strains was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation] Atlas conservation class strongly predicted within-species prevalence: 77.3% of atlas-defined Core AMR genes were fixed, 78.7% of Singleton genes were rare, and Auxiliary genes were 57.3% variable and 42.7% rare. [src: amr_strain_variation]

### Mobile and accessory defense as a comparator

The SNIPE survey extends the core–accessory comparison beyond AMR catalogs. DUF4041, the diagnostic SNIPE-associated domain, occurred in **4,572 gene clusters** from **1,696 species** spanning **33 bacterial and archaeal phyla**. [src: snipe_defense_system] Of these clusters, **13.3%** were core, **30.7%** were accessory, and **56.1%** were singleton, giving a combined accessory-plus-singleton fraction of **86.7%**. [src: snipe_defense_system]

The most represented SNIPE-containing phyla were Pseudomonadota with 556 species, Actinomycetota with 334, Bacillota_A with 275, Bacillota with 206, and Bacteroidota with 114. [src: snipe_defense_system] The broad, patchy distribution is compatible with a [[concepts/two-speed-genome]] model in which defense functions are more variable than conserved cellular machinery, although direct transfer events were not reconstructed. [src: snipe_defense_system]

SNIPE provides a mechanistically distinct contrast with AMR. It is reported to localize at the inner membrane and cleave phage DNA as it passes through the ManYZ mannose-transporter pore. [src: snipe_defense_system] Independent *E. coli* Fitness Browser measurements show that ManXYZ disruption is costly on D-mannose and D-glucosamine: across 168 experiments, the worst fitness scores were -3.93 for *manX*, -3.82 for *manY*, and -4.14 for *manZ*. [src: snipe_defense_system]

The strongest average defects were observed on D-glucosamine, with fitness values of -3.79, -2.79, and -3.63 for *manX*, *manY*, and *manZ*, respectively, and on D-mannose, with values of -2.75, -3.00, and -2.74. The mutants were approximately normal on D-fructose, while *fruA* showed the opposing fructose-specific defect. [src: snipe_defense_system] These data support the interpretation that ManXYZ is mannose/glucosamine-specific rather than a fructose transporter, and illustrate a [[concepts/condition-dependent-essentiality]] trade-off between phage resistance and nutrient acquisition. [src: snipe_defense_system]

Published coevolution experiments found that *man* mutants exceeded 95% frequency under phage pressure but declined when phages evolved alternative entry routes. [src: snipe_defense_system] SNIPE therefore suggests the hypothesis that a defense system can preserve a useful transporter while avoiding the metabolic cost of receptor loss; this transporter-preserving phenotype was not directly measured in the pangenome survey. [src: snipe_defense_system]

SNIPE was detected in *Klebsiella*, where three 558-amino-acid DUF4041 proteins co-occurred with ManYZ-related PTS annotations. [src: snipe_defense_system] This is consistent with the evolutionary rationale that SNIPE is retained where a mannose-transporter pore provides a phage entry route worth defending, but no *Klebsiella* SNIPE or ManYZ fitness data were available. [src: snipe_defense_system]

### Metal fitness and metal-specificity as a comparator

The metal-fitness atlas reveals that the AMR pattern should not be generalized to all metal-associated or stress-associated genes. Across 22 organisms and 14 metals, genes with significant metal fitness defects were **87.4% core** versus 76.9% core for baseline genes (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162). Twenty-one of 22 organisms showed positive enrichment, and the result remained robust after excluding duplicate *Pseudomonas fluorescens* strains (OR=2.065, p=5.9e-141 across 18 organisms). [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

Essential-metal fitness genes had a larger mean core-fraction delta than toxic-metal genes (+0.148 versus +0.081; Mann–Whitney U=39, p=0.015, one-sided). Manganese showed the strongest enrichment (+0.198, with all 30 important genes core), followed by zinc (+0.151), molybdenum (+0.148), tungsten (+0.145), and iron (+0.116). Cadmium and uranium were not significant, with one and two organisms represented, respectively. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The metal-specificity analysis separates these broad fitness effects from genes that are preferentially important under metals. Of the 7,609 analyzed records, 54.9% were metal-specific using a 5% non-metal sick-rate threshold; 38.0% were general sick and 7.2% were metal+stress. At a 2% threshold, approximately 41% were metal-specific, while at 10% approximately 67% were metal-specific. [src: metal_specificity]

Per-metal specificity ranged from **60.6%** for manganese and **60.5%** for molybdenum to **21.9%** for iron. Tungsten was 56.5% metal-specific, selenium 46.4%, and toxic metals ranged from 32.7% for mercury to 55.9% for cadmium. [src: metal_specificity]

Metal-specific genes remained core-enriched, but less strongly than general sick genes. Across 22 organisms with pangenome links, their pooled core fraction was **84.8%** (2,969/3,500) and their organism-mean core fraction was **88.0%**, compared with **90.2%** for general sick genes and **81.1%** for the baseline organism mean. The difference between metal-specific and general-sick enrichment was significant by a Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test (p=0.011). [src: metal_specificity]

The apparent contrast is explained by phenotype definition. The broad metal atlas includes every gene whose disruption causes a metal fitness defect, including conserved cell-envelope, DNA-repair, protein-quality-control, and central-metabolism functions. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The metal-specificity analysis indicates that specialized metal-resistance mechanisms are modestly more likely to occur in the accessory genome than general cellular functions, although the specialized set is still overwhelmingly core. [src: metal_specificity] By contrast, AMR atlases primarily enumerate annotated resistance determinants, which are more likely to represent specialized or acquired functions. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

Metal-specific genes were 1.64x more likely than general sick genes to match metal-resistance keywords (12.2% versus 7.8%; Fisher exact OR=1.64, p=2.4e-8). [src: metal_specificity] Three candidates showed strong metal-specificity across multiple organisms: UCP030820, YebC, and DUF1043/YhcB. [src: metal_specificity] YfdZ and the Mla/Yrb system were more pleiotropic, while DUF39 showed 0% metal-specificity and a mean sick rate of 0.637. [src: metal_specificity]

The metal-specificity study found zero metal-specific ICA modules because per-module z-normalization compressed most metal-experiment scores below |z|=2.0. It recommends using precomputed z-scores from the Metal Fitness Atlas, which previously identified 600 metal-responsive module records. [src: metal_specificity]

### Fitness and conservation are partly decoupled

The DvH field-versus-lab analysis provides evidence that conservation tracks fitness importance more reliably than ecological labeling. Among 2,725 non-essential genes with fitness and pangenome data, 76.3% were core overall. Genes important under field-stress conditions were 83.6% core (OR=1.58, FDR q=0.026), field-core genes were 82.4% core (OR=1.46, q=0.026), and lab-nutrient genes were 81.4% core (OR=1.36, q=0.037). Lab-specific genes were 96.0% core versus 88.5% for field-specific genes, but that comparison was not significant (Fisher exact OR=0.32, p=0.27). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The same analysis found that lab-antibiotic genes were 73.4% core and heavy-metal-important genes were 71.2% core, below the 76.3% baseline, although neither depletion was statistically significant after the reported testing. Heavy-metal genes remained the least conserved class across fitness thresholds from -1 to -3. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] These classes concern DvH fitness-important genes and should not be treated as direct AMR catalogs. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

In the Fitness Browser analysis, core and accessory AMR genes showed virtually identical non-antibiotic fitness distributions. Core genes had mean knockout fitness of −0.024, accessory genes also had mean knockout fitness of −0.024, Cohen’s d = 0.002, and Mann–Whitney p = 0.33. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The AMR atlas fitness cross-reference likewise found no substantial laboratory burden: AMR genes had a median fitness of -0.007 versus -0.012 for the non-AMR baseline (Mann–Whitney p=3.7e-6), while beta-lactamases had a median of -0.001. Singleton AMR genes were more costly, with a median of -0.019. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The *Methanococcus maripaludis* SNIPE homologue provides a limited functional comparator. Locus MMJJ_RS01635 contained both PF13250 and PF13455 and had 129 Fitness Browser experiments; its minimum fitness was -1.16 on formate/acetate, and it was dispensable under most tested conditions. [src: snipe_defense_system] Because this organism is an archaeon, these data demonstrate a measurable phenotype in a SNIPE-bearing organism but do not directly reproduce the Enterobacterales phage-lambda system. [src: snipe_defense_system]

## Interpretation

The environmental-resistome report interprets core AMR as more consistent with chromosomally encoded or intrinsic resistance and accessory AMR as more consistent with acquired, mobile resistance. Clinical enrichment of accessory AMR is compatible with [[concepts/horizontal-gene-transfer]] and antibiotic selection, but the analysis did not directly measure mobility. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas supports this intrinsic–acquired distinction in some mechanisms: highly conserved beta-lactamases and intrinsic efflux systems contrast with fully accessory examples such as blaTEM, tet(C), and ant(2'')-Ia. Conservation remains an indirect proxy because plasmid, transposon, integron, and chromosomal locations were not established for the analyzed genes. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The combined AMR, metal, and SNIPE results support a qualified multi-layer model. A conserved [[concepts/shared-stress-biology]] layer contains general cellular functions required under many stresses; a specialized but often core-enriched layer contains dedicated or conditionally important metal functions; and a more variable layer contains many acquired AMR and mobile defense determinants. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, metal_specificity, amr_pangenome_atlas, snipe_defense_system] This model is also consistent with [[concepts/condition-dependent-essentiality]], because the same core machinery can become especially important under metal stress without being a dedicated resistance determinant. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The DvH results qualify a field-versus-lab interpretation. Field-stress and field-core genes were significantly more conserved than baseline, but lab-nutrient genes showed comparable enrichment, and field-specific genes were not more conserved than lab-specific genes. Logistic regression produced cross-validated AUCs of 0.517 for field fitness, 0.531 for lab fitness, and 0.548 for combined field and lab fitness; a full model including gene length reached 0.645. These results suggest the hypothesis that general functional importance, rather than ecological context alone, is the stronger determinant of conservation in this DvH dataset. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The strain-variation report adds an important qualification: putatively acquired, non-core AMR genes showed stronger phylogenetic signal than core genes. Across 1,261 species, 701 (55.6%) had significant AMR signal after FDR correction; the median Mantel r for all AMR was 0.247, and 87.8% of species had positive correlations between ANI distance and AMR Jaccard distance. Non-core genes had a median Mantel r of 0.222 versus 0.117 for core genes; the paired comparison was highly significant (t = -8.35, p = 7.0e-16, n = 489). [src: amr_strain_variation]

This suggests the hypothesis that, after a lineage acquires a resistance element, it may be maintained and vertically transmitted within that lineage rather than distributed randomly by ongoing horizontal transfer. The inference is not definitive because near-universal core genes have little Jaccard variation, which can suppress Mantel statistics, and transfer or integration events were not reconstructed. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The fitness-cost results qualify the intrinsic–acquired interpretation. Core and accessory AMR genes had nearly identical fitness distributions, suggesting the hypothesis that transferred resistance may be cost-optimized in donor lineages or rapidly compensated in recipients. Neither mechanism was directly tested. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This connects core/accessory resistance with [[concepts/compensatory-evolution]] without establishing compensation as the explanation. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Mechanism composition follows the ecological distinction. Metal resistance represented **44.0%** of AMR in soil species and **45.0%** in aquatic species, but only **19.7%** in clinical species and **6.1%** in human-gut species. Target modification represented **27.5%** of clinical AMR and **43.6%** of human-gut AMR, compared with **10.1%** in soil and **6.2%** in aquatic species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas confirms that its catalog includes substantial non-antibiotic stress resistance. COG V (Defense mechanisms) was enriched 7.05-fold in AMR clusters and COG P (Inorganic ion transport) was enriched 1.93-fold; mercury and arsenic families were prominent. Because the AMRFinderPlus Reference Gene Catalog has broad scope, core/accessory patterns should not automatically be interpreted as antibiotic-only patterns. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The fitness analysis independently found that mechanism predicted conservation status even though conservation status did not predict fitness cost. Metal-resistance genes were 44% accessory, compared with 13% of efflux genes and 16% of enzymatic-inactivation genes; the mechanism-by-conservation association was significant (χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³). [src: amr_fitness_cost] This decoupling suggests that genomic distribution and laboratory maintenance cost are shaped by partly distinct processes, including acquisition history, ecological selection, and possible compensation. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

SNIPE-bearing species also differed from non-SNIPE species in 22 of 64 AlphaEarth dimensions after Bonferroni correction. The largest effect was dimension A19 (Cohen's d = 0.26, p = 5.0e-14), with overall effects of |d| = 0.11–0.26. [src: snipe_defense_system] The result indicates a modest environmental bias rather than strict habitat segregation, and interpretation is limited because AlphaEarth coverage was 28.4% and biased toward environmental isolates with geographic metadata. [src: snipe_defense_system]

The PhageFoundry strain-modelling data provide context for the SNIPE mechanism. Across 188 *E. coli* strains and 96 phages, the Lambdavirus phage 411_P1 infected only one strain, or 0.5%, while Myoviridae, Podoviridae, and Siphoviridae infection rates were 43.4%, 13.0%, and 9.7%, respectively. [src: snipe_defense_system] The model achieved AUC = 0.883 and accuracy = 84.3%, and its emphasis on adsorption factors is consistent with SNIPE acting at the phage entry interface, although the dataset did not directly test SNIPE genotype or activity. [src: snipe_defense_system]

## Within-species variation

The strain-variation report shows that core/accessory status is not merely a species-level label: most AMR occurrences vary among strains within the same species. The median pairwise AMR Jaccard distance was 0.435, and more than 90% of AMR gene-species occurrences were variable or rare rather than fixed. [src: amr_strain_variation]

This variation was structured into [[concepts/resistance-islands]]. The survey detected 1,517 islands across 705 species, with a mean island size of 6.2 genes, a maximum of 43 genes, and a mean phi coefficient of 0.827. Eighty-eight percent contained genes from multiple resistance mechanisms; efflux occurred in 954 islands and enzymatic inactivation in 698. Linkage supports coordinated inheritance but does not prove co-selection or functional synergy. [src: amr_strain_variation]

AMR variation also tracked phylogeny in most tested species. Closely related strains tended to share more resistance genes, including non-core genes often treated as acquired. This strengthens the connection with [[concepts/phylogenetic-amr-structure]] and redirects a simple intrinsic-versus-acquired model toward one that distinguishes acquisition from subsequent lineage-restricted maintenance. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Of 974 species with at least 15 genomes, 190 (19.5%) formed at least two distinct AMR ecotypes using UMAP and DBSCAN clustering, with a median silhouette score of 0.620. Environmental association testing was underpowered because 52.7% of genomes lacked a classifiable isolation source; only two species met the criteria for chi-squared testing. Case studies of [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], [[entities/staphylococcus-aureus]], and [[entities/salmonella-enterica]] nevertheless showed visible environmental structuring. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Among 823 species represented by genomes from at least two environments, the fraction of clinical genomes correlated with total AMR cluster count (Spearman rho = 0.465, p = 2.2×10⁻⁴⁵). Clinical-dominated species averaged **72.9 AMR clusters**, compared with **16.4** in environmentally dominated species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Their grouped accessory fraction was also higher (**93.6% vs 81.7%**, MWU p = 0.004), although the continuous correlation between clinical-genome fraction and accessory percentage was not significant (rho = 0.065, p = 0.064). [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

AMR density was concentrated in particular taxonomic groups. Klebsiella averaged 206 AMR clusters per species, followed by Salmonella (198), Citrobacter (134), and Enterobacter (93); Gammaproteobacteria contained 45% of all AMR clusters (37,752/83,008). Within phyla, pangenome openness correlated positively with AMR count in 8 of 10 tested phyla, but the overall correlation was near zero (rho=0.006), indicating strong phylogenetic structure in the aggregate relationship. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

*Klebsiella pneumoniae* had 1,115 AMR gene clusters, of which 7 were core and 1,108 accessory. *Staphylococcus aureus* had 642 clusters, with 9 core and 633 accessory; *Salmonella enterica* had 836 clusters, with 11 core and 825 accessory. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

These patterns do not show that accessory AMR is intrinsically more costly. In the Fitness Browser analysis, all 25 tested organisms showed a positive relative fitness shift for AMR knockouts versus non-AMR knockouts, with a pooled effect of **+0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098]**, but core and accessory AMR genes had indistinguishable fitness distributions. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The SNIPE survey similarly suggests that defense genes can be distributed broadly across strains and taxa without being core within species. However, the reported 86.7% non-core fraction describes DUF4041-containing gene clusters at the pangenome level, not strain-level transmission or measured defense activity. [src: snipe_defense_system]

## Relation to pangenome analysis

Core/accessory AMR is a pangenome-level property rather than a simple species-level resistance label. It is useful for studying [[concepts/organism-specificity]], [[concepts/horizontal-gene-transfer]], [[concepts/phylogenetic-confounding]], [[concepts/resistance-islands]], and the relationship between gene content and ecological niche. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_strain_variation]

The metal atlas shows why pangenome conservation must be interpreted alongside phenotype. Its 559 metal experiments across 31 organisms yielded 383,349 gene × metal records and 12,838 metal-important records; 1,182 ortholog groups had metal phenotypes in at least two organisms, and 149 conserved candidates lacked complete functional annotation. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The metal-specificity follow-up analyzed 7,609 of those records after locus-ID matching, leaving 40.7% of the atlas records unavailable for classification. [src: metal_specificity]

The same atlas scored 27,702 pangenome species using a 1,286-term metal signature. After genome-size normalization, *Leptospirillum* ranked at the 91st percentile, *Acidithiobacillus* at the 77th, *Marinobacter* at the 75th, and *Sulfobacillus* at the 71st, but bioleaching genera were not significantly enriched as a group (Mann–Whitney p=0.17). Simple repertoire presence/absence therefore did not predict metal tolerance reliably. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] This is consistent with [[concepts/phenotype-resolution-matching]] and [[concepts/latent-metabolic-capabilities]]: gene presence, conservation, and measured phenotype are related but distinct quantities.

Metal-responsive ICA modules were core-rich, with mean core fraction 0.826 among 183 modules with conservation data, but the follow-up specificity analysis failed to identify metal-specific modules because of inappropriate z-normalization. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, metal_specificity]

The DvH field-versus-lab study reinforces this point. Of 52 ICA fitness modules, field activity was not significantly correlated with module conservation (Spearman rho=0.071, p=0.62), although 21 field-active conserved modules had a mean core fraction of 0.980 versus 0.516 for nine low-field-activity lab modules. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The SNIPE analysis adds a domain-annotation example of [[concepts/annotation-gap]]. The canonical GIY-YIG family is PF01541, but the SNIPE nuclease is PF13455/Mug113, a distinct family within the GIY-YIG clan. Zero gene clusters contained both DUF4041/PF13250 and PF01541, whereas 54 DUF4041 clusters carried a Mug113-related description. [src: snipe_defense_system] Searching for the wrong family would therefore underestimate SNIPE prevalence or incorrectly classify unrelated restriction and DNA-repair proteins as SNIPE homologues. [src: snipe_defense_system]

The strain-variation results show that pangenome openness does not have a straightforward relationship with AMR variability. AMR variability weakly anti-correlated with pangenome openness (Spearman rho = -0.193, p = 2.2e-12), likely because open-pangenome species accumulate many rare or singleton AMR genes below the 5% threshold. This is threshold-dependent and should not be interpreted as evidence that open pangenomes biologically suppress AMR diversity. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Core/accessory status is not an absolute biological classification. A gene may appear accessory because it is genuinely variable, because sampled genomes do not represent the species evenly, or because too few genomes are available for a precise prevalence estimate. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Fitness Browser species had a median of 9 GTDB genomes, with a range of 2–399, so sparse sampling can label a gene core within the available sample even if it is accessory at larger sampling depth. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The strain-variation study analyzed 1,305 species and 180,025 genomes, but its ecotype and phylogenetic analyses had additional selection limits: 974 species had sufficient genomes for ecotype clustering and 1,261 had Mantel tests. Species with more than 500 genomes, including [[entities/escherichia-coli]] with 15,388 genomes and [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]] with 14,240 genomes, were excluded from ANI-based phylogenetic analysis because of a computational cap. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The atlas census covered 83,008 AMRFinderPlus hits on gene-cluster representatives, spanning 1,939 AMR gene families and 2,079 AMR products across 14,723 species. Mechanism labels were generated by keyword matching rather than CARD ontology mapping, leaving 22.2% of hits in Other/Unclassified; this limits direct comparison of mechanism-specific conservation fractions. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The SNIPE pangenome survey also has coverage and specificity constraints. EggNOG Pfam annotations may miss divergent SNIPE homologues, and DUF4041 can occur as a secondary domain in proteins whose primary annotations are histidine kinases or seryl-tRNA aminoacylation proteins. The high-confidence T5orf172/DUF4041 descriptions represented 3,675 of 4,572 clusters, or 80.4%. [src: snipe_defense_system]

## Tensions and limitations

- **Core does not prove intrinsic, and accessory does not prove mobile.** Neither the AMR surveys, the metal atlas, nor the SNIPE survey directly established plasmid, transposon, integron, or chromosomal location for the analyzed genes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas, metal_fitness_atlas, snipe_defense_system]
- **Broad metal fitness and annotated AMR produce different conservation patterns.** Metal-important genes were 87.4% core, whereas AMR genes were 30.3% core. The difference likely reflects inclusion of general cellular functions in the metal phenotype, but direct mechanism-matched comparison remains necessary. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, amr_pangenome_atlas]
- **Metal-specific genes narrow but do not eliminate this tension.** They were 88.0% core by organism mean and significantly less core-enriched than general sick genes (CMH p=0.011), indicating that specialized metal resistance has an accessory contribution but remains mostly conserved. [src: metal_specificity]
- **Accessory status does not imply greater fitness cost.** Core and accessory AMR genes had nearly identical fitness distributions (Cohen’s d = 0.002, p = 0.33). [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- **Fitness importance is not equivalent to AMR status.** DvH heavy-metal and lab-antibiotic fitness classes were not direct AMR catalogs and included broad stress-response functions. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- **Non-core phylogenetic signal is partly metric-dependent.** Near-universal core genes produce little Jaccard variation and can suppress distance-based statistics independently of biological constraint. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- **SNIPE domain detection is imperfect.** PF13250 is strongly associated with SNIPE, but some DUF4041 hits may be non-SNIPE proteins; PF13455 may be missed, and PF01541 is not the correct nuclease-family marker. [src: snipe_defense_system]
- **Coverage is uneven.** The metal atlas covered 22 of 48 organisms in its conservation analysis, and the metal-specificity analysis excluded ANA3, Dino, Keio, MR1, Miya, PV4, and SB2B because of locus-ID format mismatches. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, metal_specificity]
- **Sampling bias may exaggerate environmental differences.** Clinical isolates are overrepresented in public databases, soil and aquatic bacteria are undersampled, and AlphaEarth coverage for the SNIPE analysis was 28.4%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_strain_variation, snipe_defense_system]
- **Metadata sparsity limits ecological inference.** In the strain survey, 52.7% of genomes lacked a classifiable isolation source, leaving only two species with sufficient data for environment-ecotype testing. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- **AMR detection is incomplete and broad in scope.** AMRFinderPlus focuses on known resistance genes, while the atlas includes stress-associated functions such as mercury and arsenic resistance; mechanism categories are not fully harmonized across reports. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_environmental_resistome]
- **Fitness comparisons have limited scope.** Fitness Browser measurements come from laboratory transposon-knockout experiments and may be affected by essential-gene censoring, polar effects, strain background, and compensatory evolution. [src: amr_fitness_cost, metal_fitness_atlas]
- **Metal dose is not normalized across organisms.** Metal concentrations varied between experiments, limiting direct cross-species comparisons of fitness effects. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]
- **Classification choices affect interpretation.** The metal-specificity result depends on a 5% sick-rate threshold, although the qualitative pattern was stable across tested thresholds; its ICA analysis and validation against the Fitness Browser `specificphenotype` table remain incomplete. [src: metal_specificity]
- **Multiple testing and classification choices affect atlas interpretation.** The AMR atlas did not formally apply Bonferroni or FDR correction, and its mechanism assignments used keyword matching. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- **SNIPE mechanism and fitness remain incompletely tested.** The proposed transporter-preserving advantage is based on the characterized mechanism plus independent ManXYZ fitness data, not on direct paired experiments in SNIPE-positive and SNIPE-negative strains. [src: snipe_defense_system]

## Open Directions

1. Link accessory AMR clusters to plasmid, transposon, and integrative-element annotations to test whether the clinical accessory fraction is physically mobile. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
2. Identify AMR clusters shared between soil and clinical species and compare sequence and genomic contexts to test for cross-niche horizontal transfer. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
3. Reanalyze deeply sampled species with per-genome environment and AMR data to determine whether clinical exposure changes AMR within species rather than only between species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
4. Combine core/accessory profiles with fitness measurements to test whether environment-enriched resistance mechanisms differ in fitness cost or compensatory adaptation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_fitness_cost]
5. Model prevalence, genomic mobility, and fitness cost jointly within well-sampled species to distinguish genuinely low-cost accessory genes from genes whose costs are obscured by sampling or compensation. [src: amr_fitness_cost, amr_strain_variation]
6. Compare resistance-gene fitness across antibiotic-free, antibiotic, and metal-stress conditions to determine whether equal baseline costs conceal environment-specific benefits. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
7. Map AMRFinderPlus results to the CARD Antibiotic Resistance Ontology and repeat mechanism-specific conservation tests under systematic classification. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
8. Use phylogenetic gain/loss models and genomic-context data to test whether accessory resistance is gained faster, lost faster, or more frequently mobile than core resistance. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_strain_variation]
9. Test whether the stronger non-core phylogenetic signal persists after controlling for prevalence, gene-family size, species sampling depth, and resistance-island membership. [src: amr_strain_variation]
10. Use curated isolation sources and collection dates to test whether AMR ecotypes and resistance-island profiles are associated with ecological niche, antibiotic exposure, or lineage-specific expansion. [src: amr_strain_variation]
11. Repair locus-ID matching for ANA3, Dino, Keio, MR1, Miya, PV4, and SB2B, then repeat specificity and conservation analyses to test whether the 88.0% metal-specific core estimate generalizes beyond the 24 included organisms. [src: metal_specificity]
12. Validate the metal-specificity classes against the Fitness Browser `specificphenotype` table and repeat the counter-ion comparison using the exact fit < -1 threshold without the |t| > 4 requirement. [src: metal_specificity]
13. Reanalyze metal-specific modules using precomputed Metal Fitness Atlas z-scores rather than per-module normalization. [src: metal_specificity]
14. Use AlphaFold predictions and targeted experiments to test metal-binding or stress-specific mechanisms in UCP030820, YebC, and DUF1043/YhcB. [src: metal_specificity]
15. Normalize metal fitness effects by concentration relative to MIC and apply phylogenetic independent contrasts to test whether core enrichment persists across taxa. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]
16. Compare AMR, metal-fitness, and SNIPE families with matched functional annotations, genomic contexts, and condition-specific phenotypes to determine whether core/accessory differences arise from catalog scope or distinct evolutionary processes. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, amr_pangenome_atlas, snipe_defense_system]
17. Test whether the 149 conserved, poorly annotated metal candidates are core cellular functions or accessory metal-specific mechanisms using functional annotation, structural prediction, and targeted RB-TnSeq experiments. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]
18. Extend the DvH field-versus-lab analysis to organisms with both environmental metadata and fitness measurements, testing whether general functional importance consistently predicts conservation better than ecological labeling. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
19. Reanalyze DUF4041-containing clusters using a high-confidence PF13250/PF13455 architecture filter, profile-HMM searches, and genomic-context information to estimate the true fraction of SNIPE proteins. [src: snipe_defense_system]
20. Perform paired experiments in SNIPE-positive and SNIPE-negative strains with intact or disrupted ManXYZ to test whether SNIPE-mediated phage resistance preserves mannose/glucosamine uptake and reduces the fitness cost of receptor loss. [src: snipe_defense_system]
21. Generate RB-TnSeq or competition data for SNIPE- and ManYZ-containing *Klebsiella* strains under phage, mannose, glucosamine, and alternative carbon conditions. [src: snipe_defense_system]
22. Integrate SNIPE prevalence with defense-island, plasmid, prophage, and genomic-neighborhood annotations to distinguish mobile carriage from lineage-restricted inheritance. [src: snipe_defense_system]
23. Test whether AlphaEarth associations remain after controlling for phylogeny, sampling source, genome quality, and geographic coverage, and identify the environmental variables represented by the strongest dimensions. [src: snipe_defense_system]
