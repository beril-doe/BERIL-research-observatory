# Module-Level Versus Lineage-Level Ecology

## Core Idea

Prophage ecology can be resolved at two organizational levels: functional modules, such as tail, head morphogenesis, and anti-defense genes, and whole prophage lineages defined by clustering terminase large-subunit (TerL) sequences. In the BERDL analysis, these levels produced different ecological patterns: module composition varied with environment beyond host phylogeny, whereas individual TerL lineages showed no significant environment-specific enrichment after correction for multiple testing. [src: prophage_ecology]

This distinction supports a model in which environmental selection and gene exchange can reshape prophage functions without requiring entire prophage lineages to become environment-specific. It is therefore related to [[concepts/mobile-genetic-elements]], [[concepts/horizontal-gene-transfer]], and [[concepts/prophage-genome-modularity]]. [src: prophage_ecology]

## Evidence for Module-Level Environmental Ecology

Across 27,702 bacterial species, prophage-associated gene clusters were assigned to seven functional modules. Packaging (A), lysis (D), and lysogenic regulation (F) were nearly universal, occurring in 100.0%, 99.9%, and 100.0% of species, respectively. By contrast, head morphogenesis (B), tail (C), and anti-defense (G) occurred in 56.1%, 55.6%, and 64.3% of species, respectively, and showed greater phylogenetic and environmental variation. [src: prophage_ecology]

A constrained permutation analysis preserving host family and genome-size quartile identified eight significant module–environment associations at FDR < 0.05. In human-associated environments, tail, head morphogenesis, and anti-defense were enriched, with log2 odds ratios of 2.21, 1.98, and 1.70, respectively. Anti-defense was depleted in freshwater (log2(OR)=-0.74) and animal-associated environments (log2(OR)=-0.24). [src: prophage_ecology]

The environmental signal was not explained solely by genome size or host phylogeny. PERMANOVA produced F-statistics of 30.04 for environment, 6.17 for phylogeny, and 212.99 for genome-size quartile, with p=0.01 for all three predictors. Environmental effects also remained significant within each genome-size quartile, with all Kruskal-Wallis tests yielding p < 6.5e-78. [src: prophage_ecology]

Independent analysis of 6,365 NMDC metagenomic samples detected 57 significant module–abiotic-variable correlations at FDR < 0.05. The strongest reported associations included packaging with pH (Spearman rho=0.519), overall prophage burden with pH (rho=0.474), overall burden with temperature (rho=0.399), and overall burden with depth (rho=0.361). Head morphogenesis, tail, and anti-defense were significant in both the pangenome enrichment and NMDC analyses. [src: prophage_ecology]

## Evidence for Lineage-Level Constraint

Clustering 38,085 TerL protein sequences at 70% amino acid identity produced 10,991 lineages. The distribution was highly uneven: 6,921 lineages, or 63%, were singletons, while the largest lineage contained 1,094 members from 869 species. [src: prophage_ecology]

No individual TerL lineage showed significant environment-specific enrichment after FDR correction across 500 tests. This negative result indicates that whole-lineage environmental specialization did not exceed the expectation generated by host phylogeny under the study's constrained null model. [src: prophage_ecology]

Nevertheless, lineage ecological breadth varied. Among 824 lineages containing at least five species, 325 were classified as specialists because they had Shannon entropy < 1.0 or a dominant environment representing >80% of members; 499 were generalists distributed across at least three environments. Specialist lineages were concentrated in animal-associated, freshwater, and marine environments. [src: prophage_ecology]

Thus, lineage breadth and lineage-level environmental enrichment are distinct measurements. A lineage may be concentrated in a small number of environments without showing statistically significant enrichment beyond the environments occupied by its bacterial hosts. [src: prophage_ecology]

## Interpretation

The combined results suggest that prophage ecology is more modular than lineage-centered. Structural and anti-defense functions can vary among environments even when TerL-defined lineage distributions remain phylogenetically constrained. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that prophage modules are exchanged, retained, or lost semi-independently, while whole-lineage distributions largely reflect host range and host evolutionary history. [src: prophage_ecology]

The contrast also illustrates [[concepts/phylogenetic-confounding]]: apparent lineage–environment associations can arise because bacterial hosts are themselves unevenly distributed across environments. The constrained permutation analysis addressed this issue by preserving host family and genome-size quartile strata, but the report does not establish that environmental selection directly caused the module enrichments. [src: prophage_ecology]

The core prophage backbone and accessory functions provide a useful mechanistic distinction. Packaging, lysis, and lysogenic regulation showed strong gene co-occurrence and high contig co-localization, whereas integration and anti-defense genes were less co-localized. Across 15 phylogenetically stratified species, lysis was significant in 13/15 tests with mean Z-score 4.45, packaging in 11/15 with mean Z-score 3.40, and lysogenic regulation in 10/15 with mean Z-score 2.16. Anti-defense was significant in 3/11 tests and had mean contig co-localization of 0.281. [src: prophage_ecology]

These organizational differences suggest that environmental ecology may act most visibly on accessory or structurally variable modules, while conserved backbone genes provide weaker presence/absence contrasts because they approach a prevalence ceiling. This connects the finding to [[concepts/prevalence-ceiling]] and [[concepts/ecological-generalism]]. [src: prophage_ecology]

## Tensions

### Module enrichment versus lineage non-enrichment

Module-level analyses detected environmental associations beyond host phylogeny, including enrichment of tail, head morphogenesis, and anti-defense modules in human-associated environments. [src: prophage_ecology]

In contrast, no individual TerL lineage showed significant environment-specific enrichment after FDR correction, although specialist and generalist lineages differed in ecological breadth. [src: prophage_ecology]

The results should therefore not be summarized as evidence that prophages are either environmentally adapted or purely host-constrained. They support a scale-dependent interpretation: functional modules can display environmental differentiation while whole-lineage distributions remain primarily explained by host ecology and phylogeny. [src: prophage_ecology]

## Open Directions

- Apply dedicated prophage detection tools to representative genomes and test whether module-level environmental associations persist after removing bacterial homologs and domesticated remnants. [src: prophage_ecology]
- Use partial PERMANOVA with environment, host phylogeny, and genome size modeled simultaneously to quantify the unique environmental component of module composition. [src: prophage_ecology]
- Compare module exchange and whole-lineage turnover using gene-neighborhood, gene-content, and TerL phylogenetic analyses to test whether accessory functions move independently of lineage backgrounds. [src: prophage_ecology]
- Analyze anti-defense genes together with host defense systems to test whether environment-specific module enrichment reflects localized microbial arms races. [src: prophage_ecology]
- Use longitudinal NMDC samples to determine whether module-level prophage burden changes through time within sites, rather than merely correlating with environments across samples. [src: prophage_ecology]
