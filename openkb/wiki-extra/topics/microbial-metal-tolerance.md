# Microbial Metal Tolerance

Microbial metal tolerance concerns how bacteria survive and adapt to elevated concentrations of metals such as copper, zinc, chromium, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury — whether in industrially contaminated soils, host tissue, or laboratory fitness assays. The BERIL corpus approaches this topic from several angles at once: genome-derived tolerance scores linked to isolation ecology, large-scale environmental metagenomics correlating metal concentrations with gene content, and comparative fitness profiling across dozens of organisms and metals. Together these projects ask a shared question — does genomic or fitness-based evidence of "metal tolerance" reflect specific adaptation to individual metals, or is it better explained by broader, less metal-specific stress biology? This hub traces that question across the corpus's environmental, functional-genomic, and cross-resistance evidence.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Genomic metal scores track contamination history, but only weakly and unevenly.** The [[concepts/environmental-metal-tolerance]] work links 42,227 of 97,334 BacDive strains (43.4%) to metal-tolerance scores across 6,426 GTDB species, with 25,089 of those strains carrying isolation-source metadata [src: bacdive_metal_validation]. Strains from heavy-metal-contaminated sites scored one standard deviation above the environmental baseline (Cohen's d = +1.00, p = 0.006, n = 10), and effect sizes tracked a contamination-intensity gradient: heavy metal (+1.00) > waste/sludge (+0.57) > all contamination (+0.43) > industrial (+0.20) [src: bacdive_metal_validation]. This is read as ecological validation of the pangenome-derived [[entities/metal-fitness-atlas]] scores, consistent with metal-tolerance genes being 88% core within species while total gene content still varies meaningfully between species [src: bacdive_metal_validation].

**The signal is phylogenetically uneven, and phenotypic confirmation is currently too thin to lean on.** The contamination association held within Pseudomonadota (delta = +0.040, p < 0.001) and Actinomycetota (delta = +0.035, p < 0.001), but was not significant within Bacillota (delta = -0.012, p = 0.285) or Bacteroidota (delta = -0.008, p = 0.456) [src: bacdive_metal_validation]. A direct phenotype comparison using only 24 matched metal-utilization records (8 positive, 16 negative) was non-significant (p = 0.14, d = -0.57), so it neither confirms nor refutes the genomic score [src: bacdive_metal_validation]. The heavy-metal comparison itself needed roughly d = 0.93 for 80% power, and the observed d = 1.00 barely cleared that bar [src: bacdive_metal_validation] — a fragile foundation discussed further in [[concepts/environmental-metal-tolerance]].

**At the community level, metals explain enormous variance — but co-contamination makes attribution to any single metal unreliable.** The [[concepts/metal-co-contamination-confounding]] analysis of 51,748 soil samples found 2,355 significant COG–metal associations across nine metals (Cu, Co, Cr, Ni, Zn, Pb, As, Cd, Hg) at FDR < 0.05, and metal concentrations explained 80% of residual COG-profile variance after conditioning on batch/project effects (R² = 0.799, p = 0.005, 999 permutations) [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. But chromium, copper, lead, and zinc co-vary in many industrial soils, and the report explicitly warns that correlated tests can make Benjamini–Hochberg correction anti-conservative, so the true false-discovery rate may exceed the nominal one [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. Copper-specific analysis (116 COGs significant among 7,566 proximity-matched samples) found cell-division and nucleotide-transport genes positively associated and energy-production genes negatively associated — plausible but not demonstrably copper-specific without partial-correlation modeling [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics].

**Cross-metal and cross-stress fitness overlap point toward a shared cellular vulnerability layer beneath metal-specific tolerance.** In [[concepts/shared-stress-biology]], 4,304 of 10,821 metal-important gene records (39.8%) were also NaCl-important across 19 organisms and 14 metals, a pattern that held (36.7%) even after removing an outlier organism, and was not explained by chloride delivery — zinc sulfate, which delivers no chloride, showed 44.6% overlap [src: counter_ion_effects]. Independently, across 317 organism–metal-pair observations from 28 organisms, 98.1% of gene-level fitness correlations were positive and 99.1% significant, with all 15 well-sampled metal pairs showing >90% sign consistency [src: metal_cross_resistance]. Genes were tiered by breadth: general-stress genes (1,484 genes, mean 92.0% core) were the most conserved, metal-shared genes (2,306 genes, 91.0% core) intermediate, and metal-specific genes (4,372 genes, 89.8% core) the least conserved [src: metal_cross_resistance] — a gradient consistent with pleiotropic stress defenses being more deeply embedded in the genome than narrow resistance mechanisms.

**Chemistry-specific structure persists on top of the shared signal, preventing a single "one stress response fits all" story.** Within *D. vulgaris*, metal–NaCl correlations ranged from r = 0.715 (zinc) to r = 0.086 (iron), and shared-stress genes were important for a mean of 4.1 metals versus 2.5 for metal-specific genes [src: counter_ion_effects]. Across organisms, Fe–Zn correlation reached r = 0.61 and Co–Ni r = 0.56, while aluminum was comparatively independent at r = 0.34, and quantitative magnitude of cross-metal correlation was only moderately conserved (mean Mantel r = 0.23) even though direction was highly conserved [src: metal_cross_resistance]. Biome-stratified analysis likewise found distinct metal–COG relationships across soil, marine, and wastewater environments, arguing against a single universal resistance program [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics].

## Tensions and Caveats

The corpus contains a live, explicitly flagged disagreement, captured in [[conflicts/conflict--counter_ion_effects--metal_cross_resistance--soil_metal_functional_genomics]]: does substantial cross-condition gene overlap support only a *general* shared stress response, or can it be read as evidence for *specific* toxicity mechanisms (envelope damage, oxidative stress, cofactor displacement)? The overlap and correlation data support the weaker, general claim robustly [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_cross_resistance], but assigning particular mechanisms requires functional tests the fitness-overlap data alone cannot provide, and the strongest toxicity-hierarchy evidence comes from a single organism (DvH) [src: counter_ion_effects]. A separate BacDive check found no correlation between multi-metal tolerance scores and metal-environment isolation (Spearman ρ ≈ -0.02, p > 0.8) in only 20 matched species — an underpowered null, not adjudication [src: metal_cross_resistance].

A second tension, in [[conflicts/conflict--field_vs_lab_fitness--fitness_effects_conservation--metal_cross_resistance]], concerns whether field/ecological relevance adds anything to conservation beyond general importance: the pan-bacterial metal-important gene set is 87.4% core [src: metal_fitness_atlas], yet the DvH heavy-metal condition-specific set is only 71.2% core [src: field_vs_lab_fitness, metal_fitness_atlas], and module-level analysis found no significant relationship between field activity and conservation at all [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]. Finally, the co-contamination confound is load-bearing throughout: the 80% variance-explained result for soil COG profiles is conditional and multivariate, not evidence that any single metal is causally responsible [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics].
