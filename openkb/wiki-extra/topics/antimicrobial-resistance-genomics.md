# Antimicrobial Resistance Genomics

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genomics asks how resistance genes are distributed across bacterial genomes, species, and ecosystems, and what that distribution implies about how resistance is acquired, retained, and paid for in fitness. This corpus brings pangenome-scale surveys (tens of thousands of species and hundreds of thousands of genomes), strain-resolved phylogenetic tests, and RB-TnSeq (randomly barcoded transposon sequencing) fitness assays to bear on the same underlying question: is AMR gene content best explained by ecology and selection, by lineage history, or by irreducible biochemical cost? The answer that emerges is "all three, in different proportions depending on scale," which is why this hub sits at the intersection of five concept pages rather than resolving into one clean model.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Ecological niche restructures the core/accessory balance of the resistome.** Across 14,723 species and 82,908 AMR gene clusters, clinical species carry both more resistance and a more mobile resistome than environmental species: clinical species average 32.4% core AMR and 67.6% accessory AMR, versus 57.1% core / 42.9% accessory in soil and 54.4% / 45.6% in aquatic species, with human-gut species reaching 80.3% accessory (Kruskal–Wallis H = 506.0, p = 4×10⁻¹⁰⁷, η² = 0.036) [src: amr_environmental_resistome]. The pan-bacterial atlas independently finds the same direction at a different aggregation scale — AMR overall is depleted from the core genome (30.3% core vs. 46.8% pangenome baseline, OR = 0.49) and enriched in the auxiliary genome (2.2×) — and clinical species again carry more AMR clusters per species (10.6) than soil (4.6), aquatic (3.9), or animal (3.0) species [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]. Mechanism composition tracks the same gradient: metal resistance dominates soil/aquatic resistomes (44–45%) but is only 6.1% of human-gut AMR, while target modification is enriched in gut and clinical resistomes [src: amr_environmental_resistome]. See [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] and [[concepts/core-accessory-resistance]].

**Resistance costs are small, remarkably uniform, and consistent with prior compensation.** Across 25 bacterial organisms, knocking out an AMR gene produced a pooled fitness shift of +0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098] relative to non-AMR knockouts, positive in all 25 organisms, even though AMR knockouts were still mildly deleterious in absolute terms (mean −0.024) [src: amr_fitness_cost]. The report reads this near-universal, modest residual cost as consistent with compensatory evolution having already trimmed larger burdens, though no compensatory mutation was directly measured [src: amr_fitness_cost]. Critically, core and accessory AMR genes show statistically indistinguishable baseline costs (both −0.024, Cohen's d = 0.002, p = 0.33), even though they differ sharply in genomic distribution — recently acquired genes are apparently no more costly than ancient ones [src: amr_fitness_cost]. The same genes flip from costly to beneficial under antibiotic exposure (57.0% of 797 gene–antibiotic observations, mean flip +0.045, p = 0.0001), with efflux genes showing the strongest environmental payoff [src: amr_fitness_cost]. See [[concepts/compensatory-evolution]].

**AMR gene content is phylogenetically structured, not randomly scattered among strains.** Within 1,305 species and 180,025 genomes, most AMR gene–species occurrences are rare (51.3%) or variable (41.3%) rather than fixed (7.5%), so strain-to-strain heterogeneity is the norm [src: amr_strain_variation]. But this variation is not noise: comparing ANI (average nucleotide identity) distance to AMR-profile Jaccard distance across 1,261 species detected significant phylogenetic signal in 701 (55.6%, FDR-corrected), with a median Mantel r of 0.247 and 87.8% of species showing a positive correlation [src: amr_strain_variation]. Non-core, putatively acquired genes show markedly stronger signal than core genes (median r 0.222 vs. 0.117, paired t = −8.35, p = 7.0e-16), suggesting that once a lineage acquires resistance it tends to keep and vertically propagate it rather than distributing it at random [src: amr_strain_variation]. See [[concepts/phylogenetic-amr-structure]].

**Much of this lineage structure is explained by tightly linked resistance islands.** The strain-variation survey detected 1,517 resistance islands across 705 species (54% of those analyzed), with a mean within-island phi coefficient of 0.827 and a striking 88% (1,343/1,517) spanning more than one resistance mechanism [src: amr_strain_variation]. Efflux genes appear in the most islands (954), followed by enzymatic-inactivation (698) and oxidoreductase genes (694) [src: amr_strain_variation]. These multi-mechanism modules give a structural, testable explanation for why resistant lineages so often carry characteristic multi-drug profiles rather than single, independently segregating genes. See [[concepts/resistance-islands]].

**Mechanism identity, not cost, predicts genomic placement.** Beta-lactamases are 54.9% core, regulatory AMR genes only 6.5% core, and named mobile examples (blaTEM, tet(C), ant(2'')-Ia) are 0% core, while intrinsic efflux systems like emhABC exceed 95% core [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]. Yet these same mechanism classes show no corresponding difference in baseline fitness cost (metal-resistance genes 44% accessory vs. efflux 13% and enzymatic-inactivation 16% accessory, but Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.89 for cost) [src: amr_fitness_cost]. Comparator systems reinforce that "accessory" is not synonymous with "AMR-like": the SNIPE (DUF4041) defense system is 86.7% accessory-or-singleton across 4,572 clusters in 1,696 species [src: snipe_defense_system], while broad metal-fitness-important genes are strongly core-enriched even though only 54.9% of metal-important gene records are metal-specific rather than generally stress-sensitive [src: metal_specificity]. This tells us pangenome location, mechanism, and fitness cost are three partially independent axes.

## Tensions and Caveats

The central unresolved question is whether the strong clinical-AMR association reflects real ecological/selective structuring or database and metadata artifacts. Clinical isolates are massively overrepresented in public genome databases, only 53.2% of AMR-carrying species in the atlas received a confident environment classification, and no significant temporal AMR trend survived correction across 513 species — undermining a simple "clinical selection is intensifying over time" narrative [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_strain_variation]. See [[conflicts/conflict--amr_environmental_resistome--amr_pangenome_atlas--amr_strain_variation]].

A second tension concerns whether pangenome conservation predicts anything beyond itself. Core and accessory AMR genes have statistically identical fitness costs [src: amr_fitness_cost], and other corpus work finds that open/closed pangenome status fails to predict environment or phylogeny effect size, while conserved genes can still be burdensome or condition-specific [src: conservation_vs_fitness, field_vs_lab_fitness]. See [[conflicts/conflict--adp1_triple_essentiality--amr_fitness_cost--conservation_fitness_synthesis]] and [[conflicts/conflict--acinetobacter_adp1_explorer--adp1_deletion_phenotypes--alphafold_msa_annotation]].

Third, whether costly, mobile-element-associated accessory genes represent decaying debris or retained adaptive cargo is unsettled: prophage-marker co-localization with AMR is weak at fine scale (only 10.4% of AMR instances within 10 genes of a marker) even though defense-system counts correlate positively with prophage burden [src: prophage_amr_comobilization, phage_defense_arsenal]. See [[conflicts/conflict--costly_dispensable_genes--ecotype_functional_differentiation--phage_defense_arsenal]]. Methodologically, all phylogenetic-signal and island results depend on AMRFinderPlus's known-determinant catalog and exclude mega-species with >500 genomes (e.g., *E. coli*, *K. pneumoniae*), and resistance-island co-occurrence cannot distinguish co-selection from mere physical linkage [src: amr_strain_variation].
